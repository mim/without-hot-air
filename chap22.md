# 22 Efficient electricity use

Can we cut electricity use? Yes, switching off gadgets when they’re not in use is an easy way to make a difference. Energy-efficient light bulbs will save you electricity too. <ins style="color:#a3540f;background:#fbeee0;padding:0 3px;border-radius:2px;font-weight:600;">When this was written, "energy-efficient" meant the compact fluorescent. What happened next was the LED: by 2020 LEDs made up roughly 48% of installed lighting in American homes and businesses — up from 1% in 2010 — and the average fitting drew about 22 W in homes. The best LED packages reached 210–240 lumens per watt in production by 2020, while a 60 W-equivalent LED bulb fell roughly 90% in price from 2008 to under $5 by 2016.</ins> [^u3][^u4][^u5]

We already examined gadgets in Chapter 11. Some gadgets are unimportant, but some are astonishing guzzlers. The laser-printer in my office, sitting there doing nothing, is slurping 17 W – nearly 0.5 kWh per day\! A friend bought a lamp from IKEA. Its awful adaptor (figure 22.1) guzzles 10 W (0.25 kWh per day) whether or not the lamp is on. If you add up a few stereos, DVD players, cable modems, and wireless devices, you may even find that half of your home electricity consumption can be saved.

According to the International Energy Agency, standby power consumption accounts for roughly 8% of residential electricity demand. [^1] <ins style="color:#a3540f;background:#fbeee0;padding:0 3px;border-radius:2px;font-weight:600;">The world acted on this: the EU's 2008 standby regulation capped new devices at 1 W (0.5 W from 2013), and its 2023 successor, in force since May 2025, tightens that to 0.5 W in standby and off mode — 0.3 W in off mode from 2027 — with networked-standby caps of 2–8 W falling to 2–7 W in 2027. Brussels expects the revision alone to save an extra 4 TWh a year by 2030. (Great Britain still applies the older 2008-era rules it kept after Brexit; Northern Ireland follows the new EU regulation.)</ins> [^u1] In the UK and France, the average standby power is about 0.75 kWh/d per household. <ins style="color:#a3540f;background:#fbeee0;padding:0 3px;border-radius:2px;font-weight:600;">The American sequel, measured a decade later: a 2015 NRDC study of smart-meter data from 70,000 northern California homes found always-on but inactive devices — standby, sleep, and left-on-but-idle gear — accounting for nearly 23% of household electricity, about $165 per home per year, $19 billion nationally: the output of roughly 50 large power plants.</ins> [^u2] The problem isn’t standby itself – it’s the shoddy way in which standby is implemented. It’s perfectly possible to make standby systems that draw less than 0.01 W; but manufacturers, saving themselves a penny in the manufacturing costs, are saddling the consumer with an annual cost of pounds.

## A vampire-killing experiment

Figure 22.2 shows an experiment I did at home. First, for two days, I measured the power consumption when I was out or asleep. Then, switching off all the gadgets that I normally left on, I measured again for three more days. I found that the power saved was 45 W – which is worth £45 per year if electricity costs 11p per unit. <ins style="color:#a3540f;background:#fbeee0;padding:0 3px;border-radius:2px;font-weight:600;">At the late-2026 Ofgem price cap of about 26p per kWh, the same 1.1 kWh a day would be worth roughly £105 a year.</ins> [^u6]

![](/assets/figure189.gif)

<span class="figurenumber">Figure 22.3</span>. My cumulative domestic electricity consumption, in kWh, each year from 1993 to 2008. The grey lines show years from 1993 to 2003. (I haven’t labelled these with their years, to avoid clutter.) The coloured lines show the years 2004 onwards. The scale on the right shows the average rate of energy consumption, in kWh per day. The vampire experiment took place on 2nd October 2007. The combination of vampire-banishment with energy-saving-lightbulb installation reduced my electricity consumption from 4 kWh/d to 2 kWh/d. <ins style="color:#a3540f;background:#fbeee0;padding:0 3px;border-radius:2px;font-weight:600;">For scale, the average American household used about 30 kWh a day.</ins> [^u8]

Since I started paying attention to my meter readings, my total electricity consumption has halved (figure 22.3). I’ve cemented this saving in place by making a habit of reading my meters every week, so as to check that the electricity-sucking vampires have been banished. If this magic trick could be repeated in all homes and all workplaces, we could obviously make substantial savings. So a bunch of us in Cambridge are putting together a website devoted to making regular meter-reading fun and informative. The website, [<span class="websitetitle">www.readyourmeter.org</span>](http://www.readyourmeter.org), aims to help people carry out similar experiments to mine, make sense of the resulting numbers, and get a warm fuzzy feeling from using less.

I do hope that this sort of smart-metering activity will make a difference. In the future cartoon-Britain of 2050, however, I’ve assumed that all such electricity savings are cancelled out by the miracle of growth. <ins style="color:#a3540f;background:#fbeee0;padding:0 3px;border-radius:2px;font-weight:600;">So far, the opposite: mean domestic electricity use per British household fell 17.8% between 2005 and 2016 and stood at about 3,100 kWh a year in 2023 — roughly a third below its 2005 peak — with DESNZ attributing the long decline to better insulation and more efficient boilers, lighting, and appliances. The gadgets multiplied, but the watts per gadget fell faster.</ins> [^u7] Growth is one of the tenets of our society: people are going to be wealthier, and thus able to play with more gadgets. The demand for ever-more-superlative computer games forces computers’ power consumption to increase. Last decade’s computers used to be thought pretty neat, but now they are found useless, and must be replaced by faster, hotter machines.

The Commission expects the revision to save an extra 4 TWh/year and 1.36 Mt CO₂eq/year by 2030. Source: European Commission, *Standby, networked standby and Off mode* (ecodesign rules page). https://energy-efficient-products.ec.europa.eu/ecodesign-and-energy-label/legislative-framework/standby-networked-standby-and-mode_en — Great Britain still applies the earlier 2008-era rules it retained after Brexit. Source: *The Ecodesign for Energy-Related Products and Energy Information (Amendment) (Northern Ireland) Regulations 2025*. https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukdsi/2025/9780348269611/pdfs/ukdsi_9780348269611_en.pdf

[^u2]: <span style="color:#a3540f;font-weight:600;">*America's idle load.*</span> NRDC, *Home Idle Load: Devices Wasting Huge Amounts of Electricity When Not in Active Use*, 2015: smart-meter data from 70,000 northern California homes; always-on but inactive devices account for nearly 23% of household electricity, about $165 per home per year (up to $440 on top-tier rates), $19 billion nationally — equal to the output of 50 large power plants. Source: NRDC press release, 7 May 2015. https://www.nrdc.org/press-releases/always-inactive-devices-may-devour-19-billion-worth-electricity-annually

[^u3]: <span style="color:#a3540f;font-weight:600;">*LEDs took over lighting.*</span> US DOE, Lighting Market Characterization, baseline year 2020 (published 2024): LEDs were roughly 48% of installed lighting units in the residential and commercial sectors, up from 1% in 2010 and 8% in 2015; average wattage per installed unit fell to about 22 W in homes and 27 W in commercial buildings. Source: US Department of Energy, *DOE Publishes New Lighting Market Characterization for Baseline Year 2020*, 21 May 2024. https://www.energy.gov/cmei/buildings/articles/doe-publishes-new-lighting-market-characterization-baseline-year-2020

[^u4]: <span style="color:#a3540f;font-weight:600;">*Half of US homes mostly LED.*</span> 47% of US households used LEDs for most or all indoor lighting in 2020, up from 4% in 2015; homes mostly lit by CFLs fell from 32% to 12%; homes mostly lit by incandescent or halogen fell from 31% to 15%. Source: US EIA, *Residential Energy Consumption Survey 2020*, reported in *Today in Energy*, 31 March 2022. https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/

[^u5]: <span style="color:#a3540f;font-weight:600;">*LED efficacy and price.*</span> Midpower LED packages with efficacies as high as 240 lm/W (cool white) and 210 lm/W (warm white) were available in production in 2020, with package prices having "dropped rapidly over the past decade". Source: US DOE, *2022 SSL Manufacturing Status & Opportunities*. https://www.energy.gov/eere/ssl/articles/2022-doe-ssl-manufacturing-status-opportunities — A 60 W-equivalent LED bulb fell roughly 90% in price from 2008 to under $5 by 2016. Source: Ofgem, *Energy price cap unit rates and standing charges*. https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/your-energy-supply/your-energy-bill/energy-price-cap-unit-rates-and-standing-charges

[^u7]: <span style="color:#a3540f;font-weight:600;">*British homes use less electricity than in 2005.*</span> Mean domestic electricity consumption per meter in Great Britain fell 17.8% between 2005 and 2016 (2016 mean: 3,781 kWh, implying roughly 4,600 kWh in 2005). Source: DESNZ, *Review of the Average Annual Domestic Gas and Electricity Consumption Levels: methodology note*, December 2024. https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/676297c3ff2c870561bde743/consumption-level-review-december-2024-methodology-note.pdf — DESNZ's ECUK 2025 notes "the long-term trend since 2000 has been a reduction in the domestic energy intensity, which can be attributed to improved insulation and more efficient boilers, lighting and consumer appliances." Source: DESNZ, *Energy Consumption in the UK 2025*, Chapter 2. https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68d28e91275fc9339a248b98/Energy_Consumption_in_the_UK_2025.pdf
